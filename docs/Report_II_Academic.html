Background

Althouse Hall is a 29,133 square‑foot academic building renovated in 2009 and certified LEED Gold. Its HVAC system utilizes hot and chilled water supplied by the Central Energy Plant, which operates on natural gas for heat and electricity for cooling, and is distributed through fan coil units in offices and air handlers in classrooms (pers. comm. Mike Kiner). Althouse also acts as a hub to other academic buildings. Classrooms are managed by a Building Automation System (BAS), an electric, centralized network designed to monitor and control heating, ventilation, and air conditioning using sensors, controllers, and software (Mid‑Atlantic Controls Corp, 2023). In fiscal year 2025, Althouse consumed 131,308 kWh of electricity, emitted 39.5 metric tons of CO2‑equivalent, and incurred a cost of $10,686. Its electricity intensity of 4.5 kWh per square foot exceeded the campus median for academic buildings (3.7 kWh/sqft) but remained below the national median for U.S. college and university buildings (EIA 2022).

We expect time of year and day of week to be the most influential variables in the final model and that temperature will have a smaller effect. Electricity use in Althouse was likely higher on weekdays, when classes and offices were active, than on weekends. Electricity use likely peaked during the fall and spring semesters, when campus activity is high, and dropped during winter and summer breaks, when most students and faculty were away except for summer programs. The weak relationship to temperature is expected because the majority of heating/cooling occurs off-site.

Results

A segmented linear regression model was created which considers daily temperature, time of year and day of the week as the predictor variables for electricity consumption. This model was able to explain over half of the variation in electricity consumption (R2=0.67).

The model showed a significant relationship between electricity consumption and time of year (F3,323 = 53.9, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference between spring and fall semester but summer and winter showed lower electricity consumption. Summer had 36% less electricity use and winter had 47% less electricity use compared to the fall semester (Figure 1).

Recommendations

The results show that time of year, day of the week and temperature above 73°F were all significantly related to electricity use in Althouse. Time of year had the largest effect; summer and winter breaks saw 36% and 47% decreases in electricity use from the fall semester respectively. Day of the week also had significant effects, especially on weekends which saw roughly 30% less electricity use than Mondays. These results align with our expectations because they show that occupancy has a large effect on electricity consumption, since electricity consumption is lower on low occupancy days. Additionally, heating and cooling is managed in Althouse by a BAS system in classrooms and an occupancy schedule in offices which further lowers electricity consumption on low occupancy days (Qiang et al., 2023). These, results show that these systems are likely working as intended. In the future, more in-depth study of occupancy patterns could be used to refine these systems or utilize them in other buildings.

Our results also align with our expectation that temperature would have a lower magnitude effect on electricity use because the heating and cooling is primarily powered off-site and only relies on electricity for air movement. However, it is surprising that the relationship between temperature and electricity consumption is only significant above 73°F, since most buildings have a break point in the model near 60°F. This might be due to the spray foam insulation on the roof of Althouse (personal communication Mike Kiner, April 2026), which is incredibly effective at stopping heat transfer and air leakage. (“Top Differences Between Spray Foam & Other Insulation,” 2026). If this is true, it would be beneficial to consider using spray foam insulation in future building projects.

Since Althouse acts as a hub, sending hot and chilled water to other buildings, it would be beneficial to determine how much electricity is used in this process. This would help to better understand electricity consumption in academic buildings. Additionally, addressing issues with submeter data and adding submeters to buildings without them will present more accurate data and will give more insight on electricity consumption by occupants and/or devices.
